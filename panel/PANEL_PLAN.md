# Multi-voter annotation panel: design and run plan

Two files, both tested offline:
- `spanpanel.py` — pooling, Dawid-Skene aggregation, calibration, adjudication queue
- `panel_adapters.py` — one uniform interface per voter, shared prompt, local grounding

Run `python3 test_spanpanel.py` to verify before you trust any output.

---

## What this is and is not

**Is:** a way to decide *where a scholar's attention is needed*, calibrated against
a small adjudicated sample, reported with intervals.

**Is not:** a replacement for human judgement. Agreement between LLMs is not
evidence of correctness. They share pretraining data, tokenisation, instruction-tuning
conventions and increasingly distilled ancestry, so their errors are correlated.
Seven models all normalising `Ulleswater` to `Ullswater` is one mistake made seven
times, and a majority vote would launder it into consensus. Your own workshop
notebook 07 says this; the panel does not repeal it.

The framing for the slide is triage, not replacement:

> Machine disagreement located the 25% of spans that actually needed a humanist.

---

## Panel composition

Compose for **error decorrelation**, not headcount. Three LLMs plus two non-LLM
systems is more diverse than five LLMs, because the non-LLM systems fail differently.

| Voter | Kind | Why it is in the panel |
|---|---|---|
| `gpt-5.6-sol` | generative, frontier | your current best condition |
| Claude (Opus or Sonnet, current) | generative, frontier, different lab | different data and RLHF conventions |
| Gemini 2.5 | generative, frontier, different lab | you have access and prior results |
| Qwen or Llama, large open-weight | generative, open weight | pinnable revision, no API drift, reproducible in ten years |
| A 7–8B open-weight model | generative, capability floor | isolates scale from family |
| `dslim/bert-base-NER`, pinned | supervised | genuinely uncorrelated error mode |
| Rules + frozen gazetteer | deterministic | precision ceiling, recall floor |

Seven voters, four of them non-generative, three frontier labs, one open-weight
pair that separates scale from family.

**Contamination check before you run.** If any holdout passage was generated by a
model on the panel, record it and exclude that model from voting on those passages.
Models favour their own outputs (Panickssery, Bowman & Feng, NeurIPS 2024).

## The two rules that make the comparison fair

1. **Byte-identical prompt and schema for every generative voter.** Per-model prompt
   tuning means you are comparing your effort per model, not the models.
   `prompt_fingerprint()` writes a hash into every output record to prove it.

2. **No model supplies character offsets.** Models return verbatim quotes; Python
   locates them. Same rule as notebook 06. A model that silently normalises
   `Ulleswater` to `Ullswater` fails grounding and is caught, rather than scoring
   as a near-miss. The per-voter quote-failure rate is itself a reportable result
   and is one of the more interesting things this whole exercise measures.

## Adjudication: pooled evaluation

You have no annotators and eleven days, so do not annotate from scratch. Run the
panel first, pool its output, judge only what is contested. This is TREC pooling
(Zobel, SIGIR 1998), thirty years old and well understood.

| Stratum | What | Volume | Your time |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Unanimous | all voters agree on span and label | random sample of 40 | ~15 min |
| B. Contested | any disagreement in extent or label | all, est. 120–150 | ~45 min |
| C. Recall floor | read passages blind, mark everything, compare to pool | 25 passages | ~75 min |

**Roughly 2.5 hours of your own time.** `build_adjudication_queue()` produces A and B.
C cannot be produced from panel output by definition and must be done by reading.

**Do not skip C.** It is the only estimate of what the entire panel missed, and it
is where historical spellings and relational phrases will surface. Skipping it means
your precision figures are conditional on the panel having proposed the span at all,
which is the known bias of pooled evaluation and must be stated either way.

## Aggregation

Use Dawid-Skene (1979), not majority vote. Majority vote weights a 7B model exactly
as heavily as a frontier model, and weights a gazetteer with 1.00 precision the same
as a model that over-proposes. Dawid-Skene learns each voter's error profile from the
disagreement structure alone, with no gold labels.

Two implementation points that cost me two failed test runs and will cost you the
same if you reimplement this:

**The latent and observed label spaces are different.** A voter can emit ABSTAIN
(proposed nothing). ABSTAIN is not something a span can truly *be*. The latent
counterpart is REJECT: the cluster is spurious. Treating ABSTAIN as a candidate true
label puts mass on an impossible class, and the aggregate then **loses to plain
majority vote** (measured: 0.928 vs 0.983).

**REJECT needs an identifying constraint.** Nothing in the vote pattern alone
distinguishes "spurious cluster" from "real label with several abstentions", and
unconstrained EM parks ~12% of mass in REJECT, turning 5-of-7 agreements into
rejections. The constraint is REJECT's own definition: if nothing is there, a voter
should abstain. `reject_kappa` (default 50) is the strength of that belief.

With both fixed, on 600 simulated items: Dawid-Skene 0.990 vs majority vote 0.983,
and on a run containing 100 genuinely spurious clusters, REJECT detection scored
precision 1.000 / recall 1.000 where majority vote cannot represent REJECT at all.

**Report accuracy and coverage separately per voter.** An earlier version took the
raw confusion diagonal and ranked the rule system *worst on the panel* because it
abstains 75% of the time. Accuracy-when-speaking and propensity-to-speak are
different properties, and the difference is exactly the rules-versus-LLM story the
keynote is about. `voter_reliability()` returns both.

## Calibration

`calibration_curve()` gives agreement threshold against precision (from the judged
subset) and coverage (from everything). Pick the operating point from the curve:

> At unanimity the panel is 98% precise and covers 71% of spans; the remaining 29%
> goes to a human.

That is empirically grounded triage rather than an assertion, and it is a genuine
methodological contribution.

**Report bootstrap intervals, never bare point estimates.** `bootstrap_ci()`. On 43
items the 95% interval around .814 is roughly [.70, .93]; on 344 items it is roughly
[.77, .86]. The first cannot support a ranking claim. Show the interval on the slide.

## Run order

| Step | Command | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Freeze the panel: voters, revisions, prompt version | today |
| 2 | Contamination check: which holdout passages were model-generated | 20 min |
| 3 | `run_panel(voters, passages, "panel_v1.jsonl")` over 400 stratified passages | ~£40–80, ~1 h |
| 4 | Repeat step 3 five times for `gpt-5.6-sol` alone, for stability | ~£10 |
| 5 | `cluster_spans` → `build_adjudication_queue` → `queue_to_jsonl` | seconds |
| 6 | Adjudicate strata A and B; read 25 passages for C | ~2.5 h, you |
| 7 | `dawid_skene`, `voter_reliability`, `calibration_curve`, `bootstrap_ci` | seconds |
| 8 | Freeze as `results_snapshot_v2.json`. No new numbers after this. | Fri 19 |

`run_panel` writes incrementally and resumes, so an exception at passage 380 of 400
costs nothing.
